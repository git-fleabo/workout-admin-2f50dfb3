# Base Strength: programme catalogue review

Reviewed 10 October 2026 for possible selectable programmes in Train & Track.

Source: Alex Bromley, *Base Strength: Program Design Blueprint*, the user's Kindle edition, ASIN B08R5J58F8. Read the named programme sections and bonus strongman progressions directly in Kindle for Web. Page references below are the reader's displayed book pages, rather than counts of browser screens. [Open the book](https://read.amazon.com/?asin=B08R5J58F8).

This is an original comparison and implementation assessment, not a reproduction of the book's workout tables. The programme descriptions summarise the source; the selection priorities and app design suggestions are our interpretation. No programmes have been installed or activated.

## The ten named programmes

| Programme | Schedule | Source structure and progression | Selection description and app implications |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Volume/Intensity** (pp. 83–85) | Three full-body days | Main-lift emphasis, with limited accessories. Squat and bench have distinct volume and intensity exposures; deadlift uses the intensity progression and overhead press the volume progression. Three-week waves build capacity before a heavier phase with declining volume and RPE-guided top sets. Waves can be repeated with increases and a reassessed max. | A relatively focused three-day strength option. Needs separate volume/intensity rules, phase changes, repeatable waves, and both percentage and effort-based prescriptions. Good first-release candidate. |
| **Powerbuilder** (pp. 86–88) | Four days, upper/lower repeated; one main lift per day | Strength and muscle-building combined. Main lifts use ascending pyramid work in the base phase, followed by heavier-first reverse-pyramid work in the peak. Bodybuilding accessories have their own volume waves; workload drops during the peak. | For someone who wants substantial accessory work alongside the main lifts. Needs different prescriptions for individual sets, separate accessory progression, and editable accessory choices. More involved than a uniform sets-and-reps template. |
| **70s Powerlifter** (pp. 89–92) | Four days, upper/lower repeated; one main lift per day | Large amounts of main-lift, variation, and bodybuilding work. Three base waves and three peak waves form an 18-week sequence before any added deloads. Base work builds sets at each rep range; peak work lowers reps and shifts variations towards heavier, more specific work. Load ranges allow recovery-based choices. | A deliberately high-volume strength and size option. Needs phase-specific variations, loading ranges, separate progression by exercise role, and optional deload insertion. Later priority because of its volume and configuration demands. |
| **Bullmastiff** (pp. 93–97) | Four days, upper/lower repeated; one main lift per day | Main lifts, variations, and bodybuilding work are balanced. The final main-lift set is a plus set: extra reps above the target determine a subsequent load increase. Three-week waves reset to their prescribed starting points. Variations and accessories build sets during the base phase; the peak reduces main-lift and variation volume while intensity increases. | A four-day option with a clear performance-feedback loop. Needs an identified final plus set, a target-rep baseline, the correct max reference, wave resets, and independent accessory rules. The displayed base and peak tables each cover nine weeks; treating this as a fixed 18-week run would be an implementation choice, with deloads handled separately. |
| **Pyramid** (pp. 98–100) | Four days, upper/lower repeated; recovery alternatives are provided | A heavy main-lift set is followed by work on a heavier-capable variation and a lighter-capable variation. Three-week waves increase intensity and can repeat with higher loads. Accessories build volume separately. Recovery options include blending exercise days, spreading the four sessions over more than a calendar week, or focusing on three lifts. | Strength with close variations. Needs distinct max references for the variations and a rolling session schedule. The programme name should not be interpreted as every set of one exercise using a conventional ascending pyramid. |
| **The Minimalist** (pp. 101–103) | Three full-body days | Few main lifts and close variations, with little or no accessory work. Hard percentage-based sets use a heavier-first approach. Base waves return to the same starting load while aiming to improve rep performance; peak waves use heavier loads and fewer working sets. | A low-volume option, rather than an easy or automatically beginner-friendly option. Needs per-set percentages, AMRAP/effort guidance, and different base-versus-peak reset rules. Useful early addition for people who prefer fewer exercises. |
| **DUP** (pp. 104–106) | Three full-body days; frequent main-lift practice | Daily undulating periodisation distributes high-, medium-, and low-rep emphases across the week. Different lifts have staggered exposures so all the demanding work does not coincide. Base work uses percentage waves; the peak uses RPE-guided top sets and back-off work derived from the selected top-set load. Accessories are limited. | A main-lift practice option with varied rep demands. Needs per-lift exposure rotation, RPE target sets, and dependent back-off loads. Should remain distinct from Heavy/Light/Medium. |
| **H/L/M — Heavy/Light/Medium** (pp. 107–108) | Three full-body days | Workload varies to permit recovery. Volume work, a reduced recovery exposure, and a top-set exposure are organised across the week; lift emphases are staggered. Base waves build capacity, while peak waves reduce volume and raise effort. Waves can repeat before progressing to the next prescription. | A three-day strength option organised around recoverable workloads. Needs exposure-specific prescriptions, repeatable waves, and RPE-guided top sets. The picker should describe the workout demands clearly rather than rely on the acronym. |
| **M/R/S — Max/Reps/Speed** (pp. 109–112) | Four training days in the supplied example | Main-lift roles rotate through maximal effort, repetition work, and lighter speed/recovery work. The base uses a four-week rotation with two repetition exposures; the peak uses a three-week rotation. Roles can be staggered across lifts so maximal squat and deadlift efforts do not coincide. Variations and accessory rules change for the peak. | A more configurable strength and meet-preparation option. Needs a rotation per lift, different base/peak cycle lengths, and coordination of demanding sessions. Later priority. |
| **Strongman** (pp. 113–117) | Three days: two gym sessions plus an event session | Gym strength work supports regular practice of pressing, walking, carrying, and loading events. The example uses lower-body and upper-body gym days with a separate event day. Three-week waves can be repeated or interleaved into a six-week structure using alternating exercises/events. Base plus-set work gives way to heavier top-set and back-off work. | A specialist option requiring suitable implements and event practice. Needs event-specific loading references, alternating weeks, and prescriptions/results that can combine load, time, distance, and reps. A separate later feature rather than an ordinary barbell template. |

## Bonus strongman methods

These are progression modules to attach to suitable exercises or event sessions, rather than seven additional complete programmes.

| Method | Source summary | App requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rest-pause density wave (pp. 119–120) | A working set contains repeated efforts separated by short rests; workload and intensity vary over the wave. | Preserve the parent set and its component efforts, with intra-set rest. |
| Clusters (p. 120) | Small groups of reps use short rests while load and the number/size of clusters vary. | Cluster structure and rest timing, distinct from ordinary straight sets. |
| Timed AMRAPs (pp. 120–121) | Work is constrained by a time window; the progression varies time and load. | A time limit alongside load and achieved reps, plus pacing notes. |
| Skill/speed work (pp. 121–122) | Repeated low-rep practice, including EMOM examples, can alternate with heavier sessions. | Interval timing, effort/technique intent, and alternating exposure rules. |
| Foot-speed wave (pp. 122–124) | For walking events, select load against a fixed distance and completion-time cap; percentage and heavier-run alternatives are also described. | Load, distance, actual time, time cap, and clear per-hand versus total load recording. |
| Max-distance carry wave (pp. 124–126) | Minimum distance constrains load selection; endurance work can progress towards shorter, heavier carries. | Minimum distance and achieved distance alongside load, with adjustable equipment increments. |
| Atlas stone step-load wave (pp. 126–128) | Build work and density with the same implement before moving to a heavier stone; also applicable to bags or kegs. | Fixed implement choices, changing set/rep structures, and timed rep attempts. |

## What Train & Track already supports

Checked against the current local checkout, including work already in progress there. This is repository inspection, not a live database or deployment audit.

- Templates already contain ordered sessions, week/day metadata, exercise slots, optional movements, percentages, effort/rest guidance, and descriptions.
- Assignments map slots to exercises and max values. The existing programme-to-Today flow, logging, progress, personal editing, and review workflow provide a usable foundation.
- Personal session rows can store different weights/reps for different sets. However, the current percentage-template renderer generates uniform sets from one percentage; it cannot faithfully generate pyramids or top-set/back-off relationships without extension.
- The personal progression types currently cover fixed targets, double progression, and a source-guidance option. The source option gives guidance; it is not an executor for Bromley's individual progression rules.
- The wider workout model has timing and distance concepts, but the current personal-programme validator admits only weight/reps, reps-only, hold, and grip-hold modes. Strongman programme support needs explicit validation, planning, and logging work rather than assuming those wider concepts already make it complete.

Relevant implementation entry points: `src/lib/programme-methods.ts`, `src/lib/programme-prescription.ts`, `src/lib/programme-progression.ts`, `src/lib/personal-programme.ts`, `src/lib/supabase-programmes.browser.ts`, and `src/lib/workout-plan.ts`.

## Shared changes needed for faithful selectable programmes

1. **Programme phases and repeatable waves.** Store phase, wave, week, exposure role, and explicit repeat/advance/reset decisions. Programme durations should reflect the source structure; do not turn every option into the same fixed 12-week sequence. Deload insertion and rolling schedules should preserve session order.
2. **Max references.** Distinguish tested or estimated one-rep max from a discounted training max and from an event-specific benchmark. Give each variation its own reference where needed. The existing generic training-max field should not silently change the meaning of the book's percentages.
3. **Set-level prescriptions.** Represent straight sets, top sets, back-offs, and final plus sets separately. Support effort targets, not just effort caps. Back-off weight can depend on the top set actually selected that day.
4. **Separate progression rules.** Main lifts, variations, and bodybuilding movements need their own rules. For Bullmastiff, the source rule increases the next working load by 1% of the reference one-rep max for each extra rep above the plus-set baseline, within the relevant wave. Rounding and resets must be explicit.
5. **Reviews that respect the selected programme.** Generate source-rule proposals with a visible explanation; retain the user's ability to review changes. General coaching should recognise that a planned volume build-up or heavy week is part of the selected programme rather than silently flatten it. Changing programme rules should be an explicit personal edit.
6. **A concise picker.** Show days per week, strength/size emphasis, expected volume, main equipment, and exercise flexibility. Preview the opening sessions and explain progression before activation. Keep prescriptions and the next workout prominent after selection.

## Suggested implementation order

This ordering is an app-development recommendation, not a recommendation that the user personally follow a particular training programme.

**First milestone: Volume/Intensity and Bullmastiff.** They create useful three-day and four-day choices and establish two important reusable progression patterns. Provide a faithful preview, exercise mapping, source references, and visible progression decisions before allowing activation.

**Next: Minimalist and Powerbuilder.** These add low-volume and accessory-rich choices and justify reusable set-level load prescriptions. Follow with H/L/M, DUP, and Pyramid once top-set/back-off relationships and rolling schedules are supported.

**Later: 70s Powerlifter, M/R/S, and Strongman.** They add more volume management, lift-role coordination, or event-specific tracking. Bonus strongman methods belong in optional session-building tools rather than the top-level programme list.

Before calling a template complete, verify the entire base/peak structure, its wave transitions, equipment substitutions, missing-log behaviour, load rounding, and each progression calculation against the source. Several templates allow repeats and choices; any chosen defaults should be labelled as app defaults rather than attributed to Bromley.
